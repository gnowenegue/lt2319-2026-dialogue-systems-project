# Margarine
<!-- Source: https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Cookbook:Margarine (CC BY-SA 4.0) -->

Margarine or oleomargarine is a synthetic butter substitute used in cooking and baking.

## Characteristics
At its core, margarine is an emulsion of water in solid fat, combined with added coloring, flavoring, preservatives, and/or emulsifiers to mimic the properties of butter. The fat component may derive from animal products or vegetable products; if the fat used is liquid at room temperature, it will need to be hydrogenated for solidity. Generally, margarine has the same base proportions as butter, with about 80–85% fat and 16–18% water, often with salt and milk solids for flavor, plus colorant to make it pale yellow. Still, however, the flavor and texture do not match those of butter. Low-fat margarines have a higher proportion of water and increased stabilizers to compensate. The melting range of margarine is similar to that of butter, at 94–98°F (34–36°C), but it does not melt quite as fast as the latter. This gives it a slightly greasier mouthfeel than butter's. Some margarines are plastic and spreadable at fridge temperatures, which can be helpful in some baking applications.

### Varieties
Because margarine is a "designer" synthetic fat product, a number of different formulations with different properties are available for different purposes. Table margarine is primarily meant to be soft and spreadable, melting fully in the mouth. These may be sold in tubs or sticks/blocks, with tub margarine being especially soft. Slightly sturdier baker's or cake margarine works well for creaming and can be used to make frostings whose texture will be stable. Its melting point is around 95°–110°F (35°–41°C). Even firmer and slightly waxier is roll-in or pastry margarine, which has a higher melting point (105–115°F / 41–46°C) and works well for laminated pastries. The firmest with the highest final melting point (115–135°F / 47–57°C) is puff pastry margarine, which creates very flaky pastries but has a waxy texture unpleasant to some.

## Selection and storage
Make sure to select the variety of margarine most suitable for your application. Margarine with sufficient salt and preservatives can be kept long-term without refrigeration. Otherwise, it needs to be stored in the fridge. Make sure to keep it away from light and moisture for the longest shelf life.

## Use
Margarine has a couple of applications, primarily as a substitute for butter when the latter cannot be used. For example, many (but not all) margarines are suitable for individuals adhering to restricted diets, such as vegans/vegetarians, those with allergies, etc. Specialty margarines designed for baking also tend to be tailored for specific structural attributes, such as greater plasticity when cold. Butter and margarine may be combined in a given recipe, but they must be the same consistency when mixed to ensure even blending.

## Substitution
Generally, it's okay to substitute margarine and butter for each other, though the end result will be slightly different. In cases where butter flavor is paramount, it's best to use it instead of margarine (dietary restrictions aside). Reduced-fat margarines cannot be used in high-heat cooking applications, since their stabilizer content causes them to scorch and fail to melt correctly. They also cannot usually be used in baking due to their skewed proportions.
